# Bastiat and Mill on Pure vs. Eclectic Utilitarianism: Study Summary

**Source:** Hunt & Lautzenheiser, *History of Economic Thought: A Critical Perspective* (2011), Ch. 8, "Pure Versus Eclectic Utilitarianism: The Writings of Bastiat and Mill" (pp. 174–201).

> **Heads up:** Hunt is a *critical* (Marxian-leaning) historian. He explains each thinker's ideas and then argues about them. This summary labels **(a) the thinker's theory** separately from **(b) Hunt's critique**. Examples marked *"my illustration"* are not in the book. Everything else is from Hunt's text.

---

## 1. The big idea in three sentences

1. By the 1840s, **socialist ideas** (many built on Ricardo's labor theory of value) were spreading fast, especially in France, so conservatives wanted an economics based on **pure utilitarianism** that showed capitalism was harmonious.
2. **Frédéric Bastiat** (*Economic Harmonies*, 1850) is the **pure** utilitarian: he pushed the utility/exchange view to its logical end. "Exchange is political economy," every exchange benefits both sides, and so laissez-faire capitalism is good for everyone.
3. **John Stuart Mill** (*Principles of Political Economy*, 1848) is the **eclectic** utilitarian: he claimed to follow both Bentham and Ricardo, but he qualified both so much that he ended up being neither. He became a reformer who sympathized with workers and even with socialism.

**Why the chapter title?** "Pure" = Bastiat applies utilitarianism consistently. "Eclectic" = Mill mixes ideas from many sources (utility *and* labor theory, laissez-faire *and* reform), which makes him more humane but less consistent.

**Hunt's family tree:**

| | Bastiat | Mill |
|---|---|---|
| Led to | Austrian school and today's Chicago school | Marshallian (moderate) neoclassical economics |
| Politics | Extreme conservatism, rigid laissez-faire | Liberal reform, government intervention |
| Style (per Hunt) | Doctrinaire, sanctimonious, arrogant | Polished, fair-minded, scholarly (like Smith) |
| Logic (per Hunt) | **Consistent** utilitarian | **Inconsistent** eclectic |

## 2. Background: the spread of socialist ideas (pp. 175–176)

Socialist *economics* came mostly from England (Thompson, Hodgskin, Owenites, see Ch. 7), but socialism grew fastest in **France** in the 1830s–40s. Hunt names four French figures:

| Who | Key idea |
|---|---|
| **Gracchus Babeuf** (1760–1797) | Nature made people equal; inequality = injustice; commerce pumps "sweat and blood" into "lakes of gold for the few." Executed after a failed plot. |
| **Henri de Saint-Simon** (1760–1825) | Condemned selfish competition and the **idle rich**; favored planned cooperative production and heavy government involvement. |
| **Charles Fourier** | Only ~1/3 of people do useful work. Workers should form voluntary cooperatives called **"phalanxes."** One of the first to predict **competition leads to monopoly**. |
| **Pierre-Joseph Proudhon** (1809–1865) | *What Is Property?* Answer: **"Property is theft."** Property = "mother of tyranny"; the state's main job is coercion. Dreamed of small farms and handicrafts. Bastiat's most hated opponent. |

**Why it matters:** Bastiat wrote specifically to *answer* these socialists.

## 3. Frédéric Bastiat (1801–1850): pure utilitarianism

### (a) Bastiat's theory

#### Science and God on his side
- **Science:** Political economy is like **astronomy and chemistry** (it *observes*). Socialism is like **astrology and alchemy** (it *imagines*).
- **Religion:** Laissez-faire is **God's order**. An atheist saying "leave it to chance" would be absurd, but believers can say "Let God's order and justice prevail."

#### Exchange is everything
- Quoting Condillac: the very fact an exchange happens proves **both sides gain**. "Every exchange represents two gains for humanity."
- **"Exchange is political economy... it is society itself."**
- Goal: prove that "all men's impulses, when motivated by legitimate self-interest, fall into a **harmonious** social pattern."

Hunt boils the utilitarian argument down to a **syllogism**:

> 1. All exchanges are mutually beneficial to all parties.
> 2. All human interactions can be reduced to exchanges.
> 3. Therefore, all human interactions are beneficial to all parties.

Compare: Smith gave exchange only a few dozen pages out of ~1,000. Ricardo said economics was about **distribution** among classes. With Bastiat, Smith's "invisible hand" pages became *all* of economics.

#### Utility, effort, and "service"
- **Self-interest** is the mainspring of action. Wants are unlimited, means are limited: "**Desire runs ahead, while the means limp along behind.**"
- Universal principle: we try to **increase satisfactions relative to effort** (basically Bentham's utility maximization).
- **Two kinds of utility:**
  - **Gratuitous utility:** free gifts of nature/Providence (like **water**). Never priced.
  - **Onerous utility:** requires human effort or pain (like **diamonds**). This is his attempt to solve Smith's **water–diamond paradox**.
- **Service** = productive effort; "effort on the part of one man, whereas the want and the satisfaction are another's." Service = **pain endured** so production can happen.
- **Labor is just one kind of service.** Landlords and capitalists also provide services and endure pain.
- **Value comes from services:** "Value is not transmitted from the material object to the service, but from the service to the material." (This reverses the direction later neoclassical theory uses; Say was actually closer to neoclassical theory here.)

#### Defense of property, capital, profit, rent
- **Property is natural and God-given:** "Property does not exist because there are laws, but **laws exist because there is property**." Man is "born a proprietor"; property is an extension of human faculties.
- **To the rich:** your fortune is not theft; it is compensation for effort, sweat, risk, skill, and sacrifice.
- **Capital** comes from three virtues: **foresight, intelligence, and thrift**. Saving means sacrificing the present for future generations.
- **Inheritance** is sacred because fathers love their children; banning bequests would "paralyze a full half of human effort."
- **Rent:** nature's part of land is free; landlords only charge for their **improvements** (clearing, draining, enclosing). So land is just immovable capital.

#### Government, freedom, and the "harmony" law
- Government should only maintain "**liberty, property, and individual rights**." Government is the main barrier to competition.
- Answer to all social problems: "Men's interests are harmonious; therefore, the answer lies entirely in this one word: **freedom**."
- **Bastiat's distribution law:** As capital grows, the interest rate falls, so
  - capitalists' **absolute** share ↑ but **relative** share ↓
  - workers' share ↑ **both** relatively and absolutely
  - → workers and capitalists have harmonious interests.

### (b) Hunt's critique of Bastiat

1. **"Service = pain" is inconsistent.** Bastiat defines service as human pain, then counts *letting others use your money, tools, or land* as service. How does owning money and collecting interest hurt? The capitalist's only "pain" is (1) not leaving the factory idle and (2) not blowing the inheritance. Hunt: a thinker's **inconsistencies reveal class bias**, because the bias is more consistent than the logic.
2. **Fake history of capital.** Workers earn subsistence and can't save millions. Real fortunes came from **fraud, coercion, conquest**, and after a generation or two, **inheritance**.
3. **Inheritance defense is about love, not self-interest**, which breaks his own self-interest theory. (Thompson had argued that only cooperation protects *all* children.)
4. **Rent claim is just asserted.** No theory against Smith/Ricardo. And the people draining and improving fields were **peasants and wage workers**, not absentee landlords in Paris mansions. So much for "observing facts."
5. **"Free" exchange for workers** = choose between starving and taking a subsistence wage.
6. **Bribery is also an exchange.** If everyone is self-interested, politicians and capitalists will trade **bribes for monopoly laws**. Both parties gain, the public loses. Bastiat ignores this exchange because it breaks his harmony.
7. **Logical error in the distribution law.** A falling profit **rate** doesn't mean a falling profit **share**. If capital grows faster (in %) than the rate falls (in %), capitalists' share **rises** and workers' share **falls**.

> *My illustration:* Capital doubles from 100 to 200 (+100%) while the profit rate falls from 10% to 8% (−20%). Total profit goes from 10 to 16. If output didn't grow 60%, capital's share went *up*, the opposite of Bastiat's "law."

## 4. John Stuart Mill (1806–1873): eclectic utilitarianism

Mill claimed to be a disciple of **both Bentham and Ricardo**, but qualified everything so heavily that "the doctrine he ended with was totally different from the doctrine that he first asserted." Hunt: **Mill was neither a utilitarian nor a labor theorist** in the end.

### 4.1 Production vs. distribution
- **Laws of production** are fixed by nature and technology ("not an arbitrary thing").
- **Laws of distribution** are **human institutions**, "partly of human institution," and can be changed.
- Private property is **not** sacred or God-given. It's "wholly a question of **general expediency**. When private property is not expedient, it is unjust."
- Exchange is **not** the heart of economics: it's like "roads and carriages," just machinery, not the law of motion.

### 4.2 Mill breaks the two axioms of Benthamite utilitarianism

| Bentham's axiom | Mill's view |
|---|---|
| 1. All motives reduce to **self-interested pleasure-seeking** | Only people shaped by competitive capitalism act mostly from self-interest. In a future socialist society, people could act from **"higher" or "nobler" motives**. |
| 2. Each person is the **sole judge** of their pleasure; no comparing kinds of pleasure ("**pushpin is as good as poetry**") | "Some kinds of pleasure are **more desirable and more valuable** than others." "Better to be a **Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied**." |

Hunt (who agrees with Mill here): if some pleasures are morally higher, there must be a principle **above** pleasure, so Mill was **not a consistent utilitarian**. This "utterly destroys" the basis of utilitarian welfare economics.

### 4.3 Mill's theory of value
- Starts with something close to **Ricardo's labor theory**: production needs labor + natural objects; cost "is resolvable into labour"; value depends "**principally**" on labor.
- Then qualifies it: labor theory only holds if **capital/labor ratios are equal** across industries. Wine (aged longer) vs. cloth example; anything made by machinery is like wine.
- **Reverts to Smith's "adding-up" cost-of-production theory:** natural price = price of land + price of labor + price of capital. Market price set by supply and demand, gravitating to natural price.
- **Two inconsistent views of profit:**
  - Ricardian: "profit arises, not from the incident of exchange, but from the **productive power of labour**" (labor produces more than its support).
  - Senior-style (the one that dominates): profit is "the **remuneration of abstinence**," plus payment for **risk** and **superintendence**. So profit arises from **exchange**.
- Value = just **exchange value** (relative price). He dismissed Ricardo's search for an invariable measure of value.

### 4.4 International trade: offer curves (one of his big original contributions)
- Ricardo showed **comparative advantage** but didn't explain how the international price ratio was set.
- Within a country, competition makes price = cost. But capital and labor **can't move freely between countries**, so international prices depend on **supply and demand**, not cost.
- Each country must **balance payments** (exports pay for imports). At each price between the two countries' cost ratios, it offers some exports for some imports → an **offer curve**.
- **Equilibrium** = where the two countries' offer curves cross. Still the main orthodox theory of international prices today.

### 4.5 Mill on wages
- In *Principles* (1848), Mill **accepted** the **wages fund doctrine**: wages = fund set aside by capitalists ÷ number of workers.
- Best way to raise wages: **education → smaller families**. Unlike Malthus, Mill did **not** see birth control as a vice.
- Unlike Senior, he thought **strikes** taught workers valuable lessons.
- **1869 recantation** (review of W. T. Thornton's book): the limit on wages is **total profits minus what capitalists need to live**. Wages are set by **struggle** between workers and capitalists, and higher wages come out of the capitalist's income. **Mill's sympathy was with the workers.**

### 4.6 Falling rate of profit and crises
- Agrees with Ricardo: accumulation + population growth → food prices and rent ↑, **profits ↓**.
- **Counteracting forces:**
  1. **Export of capital** to colonies and abroad (similar to later Marx and **Lenin** on imperialism). Brings back cheap food and materials. "The more capital we send away, the more we shall possess."
  2. **Commercial crises** that **destroy capital** through over-trading and "rash speculation."
- He described **business cycles** clearly: overbuilding → crisis → layoffs → impoverishment.
- **But he still defended Say's Law** (no general glut):
  - *Definitional move:* a "glut" is really an "**undersupply of money**" (Hunt: that's exactly what Malthus meant).
  - *Theoretical move:* crises are an evil but **temporary**; markets recover in the long run.
  - Keynes's reply to this kind of argument: "**In the long run we are all dead.**"

### 4.7 Mill on socialism
- Modern European property came from "**conquest and violence**," not fair division or hard work.
- Rejects any "class" that doesn't labor (except those unable to, or who earned rest).
- "The division of the human race into two hereditary classes, employers and employed," **can't last forever**.
- If the choice were Communism vs. the current system where reward runs in **inverse proportion to labor**, then Communism's difficulties would be "**but as dust in the balance**."
- **But** socialism is "at present workable only by the **elite of mankind**." Until characters improve, "while minds are coarse they require **coarse stimuli**" (the struggle for riches).
- Favors voluntary **cooperatives** (Owen, Fourier). Over time, worker–capitalist **partnerships** and then **associations of workers themselves** could replace master/worker relations.
- Near-term goal: **improve** private property, not overthrow it.

### 4.8 Mill's interventionist reforms
Laissez-faire "should be the general practice," **but** "society is fully entitled to abrogate or alter any particular right of property" that blocks the public good. Government's ends are "as comprehensive as those of social union."

Three problem areas:
1. Mass **poverty** and endless toil
2. A small minority born to **unearned wealth**
3. **Monopoly** and collusion

His reforms:

| Reform | Details |
|---|---|
| **National education** for working-class children | Paired with ending extreme poverty for a whole generation |
| **Public money for colonization** + common land for the poor | Funded from surplus capital that would be sent abroad or wasted |
| **Labor protections** | Right to form unions; child labor limits; **limits on working hours** (one worker can't bargain alone); factory safety |
| **Poor relief** | Minimum subsistence, but on the **1834 Poor Law** principle (everyone should have a strong motive to avoid it) |
| **Inheritance tax / cap** | His main tool against extreme wealth: limit what anyone can receive by gift or bequest |
| **Regulate or publicly capture natural monopolies** | Government should set conditions or take the monopoly profits for the public |

Key anti-utilitarian line: "The person most interested is **not [always] the best judge** of the matter." So laissez-faire's foundation "breaks down entirely."

### 4.9 (b) Hunt's critique of Mill

1. **Eclectic and inconsistent** in both value theory and profit theory.
2. **Surplus-capital inconsistency:** if there's always surplus capital to fund reforms for a whole generation, how can profit be needed as a reward for abstinence? Hunt: Mill was a humanitarian but couldn't fully drop the **ideological justification of profit**.
3. **Utilitarianism can't support reform** (so Mill had to leave it):
   - **Unanimity problem:** if good = subjective pleasure, you can't compare people, so you can only approve changes **everyone** wants. One **misanthrope** (who enjoys others' pain) breaks the social ethic, and "pushpin is as good as poetry" means you can't rank the philanthropist above the misanthrope.
   - **Self-interested politicians:** if everyone is selfish, politicians will trade laws for money from the rich. Reform becomes **impossible**.
4. Because Mill rejected utilitarianism, reform was only **improbable**, not impossible: he could hope for a **public-spirited politician**. But in capitalism, **money is power and power begets money**.
5. Mill hoped the rich would become "**content with being rich**" and give up political privileges. Hunt: in the 162 years since, they "seldom" have.

## 5. Side-by-side comparison

| Topic | Bastiat (pure) | Mill (eclectic) |
|---|---|---|
| Main book | *Economic Harmonies* (1850) | *Principles of Political Economy* (1848) |
| Heart of economics | **Exchange** ("exchange is political economy") | Production + distribution; exchange is just "machinery" |
| Private property | **Natural, God-given, sacred** | **Human convention**; just only if expedient |
| Origin of property | Thrift, foresight, effort | "Conquest and violence" |
| Value | From utility of **services** | Mostly labor at first → **adding-up cost of production** |
| Profit | Reward for capitalist's **pain/service** | Both surplus of labor *and* **abstinence + risk + superintendence** |
| Rent | Only for landlord's improvements | (Follows Ricardo on rising rent with accumulation) |
| Pleasures | Self-interest is the mainspring | Some pleasures are **higher**; Socrates > fool |
| Class relations | **Harmony** | Two hereditary classes can't last |
| Socialism | The enemy (Proudhon) | Morally better than current capitalism, but needs better people first |
| Government | Only protect liberty, property, rights | Education, labor laws, inheritance tax, regulate monopoly |
| Workers | Free to choose; their share rises | Sympathy with workers; unions and strikes useful |
| Legacy | **Austrian & Chicago** schools | **Marshallian** neoclassical, liberal reform |

## 6. Key terms

- **Pure utilitarianism:** consistently applying Bentham's pleasure/self-interest principle to all of economics (Bastiat).
- **Eclectic:** mixing ideas from different sources, often inconsistently (Mill).
- **Economic Harmonies:** Bastiat's 1850 book claiming all class interests harmonize under laissez-faire.
- **Gratuitous utility:** usefulness given free by nature (water). Unpriced.
- **Onerous utility:** usefulness that requires human effort/pain (diamonds). Priced.
- **Service (Bastiat):** productive effort/pain by one person to satisfy another's want.
- **Unanimity requirement:** utilitarianism can only say a change is better if *no one* is worse off.
- **"Property is theft":** Proudhon's slogan.
- **Phalanxes:** Fourier's voluntary cooperatives.
- **Laws of production vs. distribution:** Mill: production is fixed by nature; distribution is set by human institutions.
- **General expediency:** Mill's test for property rights: keep them only if they serve the general good.
- **Pushpin vs. poetry:** Bentham: equal pleasure = equal value. Mill disagreed.
- **"Socrates dissatisfied":** Mill: quality of pleasure matters, not just quantity.
- **Adding-up cost-of-production theory:** natural price = wages + profit + rent (Smith's, revived by Mill).
- **Remuneration of abstinence:** Senior's term for profit, adopted by Mill.
- **Offer curve:** quantities a country will export/import at each price while balancing payments; equilibrium where two curves cross.
- **Wages fund doctrine:** wages = fund set aside for labor ÷ number of workers. Mill accepted it in 1848, **recanted in 1869**.
- **Export of capital:** sending capital abroad to keep domestic profit rates up (Mill; later Marx, Lenin).
- **Commercial crisis / revulsion:** periodic busts that destroy capital.
- **Say's Law:** supply creates its own demand; no general glut. Mill defended it despite describing crises.
- **Natural monopoly:** an industry that can't be competitive; Mill wanted government to regulate it or take its profits.

## 7. Likely exam questions (with short answers)

1. **Why does Hunt call Bastiat "pure" and Mill "eclectic"?** Bastiat consistently pushed utilitarianism and exchange to their conclusion (harmony, laissez-faire). Mill mixed Bentham and Ricardo, then qualified both until he rejected key parts of each.
2. **What syllogism sums up utilitarian economics?** All exchanges benefit all parties; all interactions are exchanges; so all interactions benefit all parties.
3. **How did Bastiat try to solve the water–diamond paradox?** Gratuitous utility (nature's free gifts, like water) vs. onerous utility (requires effort, like diamonds). Only onerous utility is priced.
4. **What is Hunt's main inconsistency charge against Bastiat?** He defines service as pain, then calls lending money or letting land be used a "service." Inconsistencies reveal class bias.
5. **What's wrong with Bastiat's distribution "law"?** A lower profit *rate* doesn't mean a lower capital *share*; if capital grows fast enough, capital's share rises.
6. **What exchange does Bastiat ignore?** Bribes for monopoly-protecting laws between capitalists and politicians.
7. **Which two Benthamite axioms did Mill reject?** (1) All motives are self-interest. (2) Each person is sole judge of pleasure / pushpin = poetry.
8. **What did Mill mean by distinguishing production and distribution?** Production is set by nature; distribution is a human institution that can be changed.
9. **How did Mill's view of wages change in 1869?** He dropped the wages fund doctrine; wages are set by struggle, limited by profits minus capitalists' living needs.
10. **What two forces counteract the falling rate of profit for Mill?** Export of capital, and commercial crises that destroy capital.
11. **Did Mill accept Say's Law?** Yes, despite describing crises; he called gluts an "undersupply of money" and said they were temporary.
12. **Was Mill a socialist?** Not exactly: socialism was morally better than existing capitalism, but only workable by the "elite of mankind" for now, so he favored reform and cooperatives.
13. **Name three of Mill's reforms.** Inheritance limits, national education, hours limits, union rights, child labor laws, monopoly regulation, colonization grants.
14. **Why can't consistent utilitarianism support reform (Hunt)?** Unanimity requirement + self-interested politicians.

## 8. How this chapter connects

- **Ch. 5 (Ricardo):** Mill claimed to follow Ricardo (falling profit rate, comparative advantage) but abandoned the labor theory of value and extended trade theory with offer curves.
- **Ch. 6 (Bentham, Say, Senior):** Bastiat develops Bentham/Say's utility approach to its extreme; Mill borrows Senior's **abstinence** theory of profit, the **wages fund**, and the **1834 Poor Law** principle, but breaks Bentham's axioms.
- **Ch. 4 (Malthus):** Mill agrees education limits family size, but sees birth control as fine, not vice; rejects Malthus's general glut.
- **Ch. 7 (Thompson, Hodgskin):** Hodgskin would agree property is natural but say *capitalist* property is unnatural. Thompson's argument about protecting all children vs. Bastiat's inheritance defense.
